DRISHTI Team Brief

Everything the team needs to explain, defend and demo DRISHTI — without memorising code. If each person can deliver Sections 1 to 3 cold, no judge can catch the team out.

Section 1
Say it cold

The thirty-second version

Everyone on the team says the same two sentences. Not similar ones — the same ones.

Say this

“Relocation in India today starts after the disaster. DRISHTI says which villages to move before the next one.”

“We take free satellite elevation, work out how often every patch of land gets hit, and rank the habitations that shouldn't be there — then say where they go, how many fit, which law applies, and which officer signs.”

If someone wants one more line, it is this one:

The closer

“If a flood comes back faster than your wall lasts, you are not protecting that village — you are rebuilding a wall it will outlive.”

Section 2
The one idea

The system runs on two clocks

If a team member understands only one thing, make it this. Every architecture question resolves here — including “how can you afford to run this nationally?”

Slow clock · computed once

The land doesn't change this week

“Where is dangerous?”

Terrain, drainage, red zones. Built from satellite elevation, baked to disk, served offline forever.

Fast clock · every 30 min

The sky changes by the hour

“Where is dangerous right now?”

Cloud structure, rainfall, soil, river response. Cheap, so it runs over the whole country continuously.

Say this

“Rain over empty ground is weather. The same rain over a district where three lakh people already live inside a red zone is an emergency. Only a system holding both clocks at once can tell those two apart.”

It is also the cost answer: a full district assessment takes seconds, so running it for 735 districts continuously is impossible. A cheap grid screens the whole country; the expensive physics is spent only where the grid fires.

Section 3
The chain

Six steps, one sentence each

Anyone should be able to walk this without notes.

  1. Read the landFree 30 m satellite elevation → work out how high every square sits above its nearest stream. That number is called HAND.
  2. Make it rainRain falls, some soaks in, the rest runs off and raises the river. We solve for the river level in metres, hour by hour.
  3. Draw the hazardsFive of them — flood, landslide, waterlogging, coastal erosion, cloudburst — each with its own published method.
  4. Find the red zonesRun all five at a 5-, 25- and 100-year storm. What comes out is how often each place gets hit.
  5. Count the peopleWho lives there, and how exposed they are — weighted with Census 2011 literacy, SC/ST share and rural share.
  6. Say what to doWhere they move, how many fit, protect-or-relocate, the legal instrument, the named authority, the SOP.
The line that makes HAND click

“A cell with HAND of two metres floods when the river rises two metres — anywhere on the map. That one number turns a river gauge into a map of whose house is underwater.”

Section 5
The maths

How each piece is actually calculated

You do not need to derive these. You need to be able to say what goes in and what comes out, and to point out that every weight is published in the app.

Runoff — how much rain becomes flood

S = 25400/CN − 254 Q = (P − 0.2S)² / (P − 0.2S + S)

The USDA Curve Number method. CN runs 30–98: concrete is about 90 because it absorbs nothing, forest on sand is under 50.

Flood depth

depth = river level at your outlet − your HAND

That is the entire flood map. Positive means underwater.

Landslide — BIS IS 14496 (Part 2)

lithology 2 + structure 2 + slope 2 + land use 2 + relief 1 + hydrogeology 1 = TEHD out of 10

The Indian national standard, not an invented index. Above 7.5 is “very high”. A state engineer already knows this scheme and already has procedure attached to it.

Coastal erosion — Bruun 1962

retreat = sea-level rise ÷ nearshore slope

Gentle slopes retreat furthest for the same rise — which is exactly why Odisha's deltas and the Sundarbans lose land fastest.

Who moves first — priority score

0.34 people exposed 0.26 how often it recurs 0.16 how uninhabitable 0.16 how vulnerable 0.08 share of settlement hit

Two villages with the same headcount and the same flood do not rank equally — the kachcha-housed one ranks higher. That is the word “vulnerable” in the problem statement, turned into arithmetic.

Where they go — site suitability

0.26 safety margin 0.22 road access 0.16 water access 0.14 buildable slope 0.12 land headroom 0.10 farmland conflict

Water access is deliberately non-monotonic — too far is a supply problem, too close is a flood problem. Capacity is 2,500 people/km² rural, 8,000 urban.

Protect or relocate

hazard returns faster than 25 years → RELOCATE

25 years is the design life of a protection structure. This single comparison is what turns a hazard map into a decision.

Cloud state — four rules, no machine learning

a level counts as filled ≥ 55% cover anvil signature high − low ≥ 25 points CAPE moderate/strong 1000 / 2500 J/kg

All three levels filled with CAPE over 2500 is deep convection. Add the anvil and it is a mature system — cirrus blowing off a storm that has already topped out.

Section 6
Drill this

Hard questions, and the answer

Run this as a drill. One person asks, another answers, out loud. The answers are short on purpose — the shortest true answer always wins the room.

Isn't this just a weather app?

A weather app tells you it will rain. We tell you that rain lands on two lakh people living exactly where the water goes. The terrain half is the half a weather app doesn't have.

Where is the AI?

Two trained models, used to make national screening fast — and deliberately kept out of the decision path.

A model that predicts rainfall is a model you have to believe. Every number that drives a decision here is a published weight over a stated threshold, and you can audit each one in the app.

Are those real river gauge readings?

No, and the app says so on every single response. There is no public, documented, keyless CWC feed — we tried three routes and wrote up what happened in the code.

River level is routed from live rainfall through real 30 m terrain and labelled modelled. A modelled stage shown as a gauge reading would be a lie with a decimal point on it.

Only 22 districts? That's not national.

All 735 are covered, in two tiers, labelled on every record. 22 have full physics; the rest get live weather screening that plainly says it is not a hazard assessment.

Promotion is a data task, not a code change — bake a district's elevation and it moves tier. The pipeline already handles all 735. What the other 713 are waiting on is disk space, not development.

How do you know the flood model is right?

We tested it against events that actually happened. Historical disasters land at a mean susceptibility percentile of 66.6 against 50 by chance, Spearman ρ = 0.46. Flood-extent detection scores F1 0.81 on real terrain.

We report those numbers whatever they say. That is why you can trust the ones that are good.

What happens if the venue Wi-Fi dies?

Everything except the live board keeps working, from baked data. Unplug it and try — that offer is part of the demo.

Hazard atlases already exist. Why build this?

Those are static PDFs at state scale, revised every several years. This is 30 m, recomputed on demand, and it goes all the way to naming the habitation and the officer who signs the order.

Relocation is politically impossible. Isn't this academic?

That is exactly why the output ends at a legal instrument and a named authority. RFCTLARR 2013, DM Act sections 30, 31 and 38, CRZ 2019, PMAY-G — the powers already exist.

We are not inventing authority. We are pointing at it, with evidence attached.

Other teams are also doing flood mapping. What's different?

They map the flood. We map how often it comes back, and compare that against how long a protection wall lasts. Recurrence versus design life is the decision — the map is just how we get there.

What data do you still need?

CWC river gauges — and that is the only one. Everything else is already live or already baked, and needs no credentials at all.

Why should a state trust a hackathon prototype?

Because nothing in it is bespoke where a standard exists. The landslide rating is BIS IS 14496. The rainfall bands are IMD's own warning categories. The cloudburst definition is IMD's. The runoff method is USDA.

A District Magistrate already has standing orders attached to those words. That is worth more than a cleverer index nobody can act on.

Section 8
Be honest

What we openly do not claim

Brief the team on these first. The fastest way to lose a technical panel is to be caught overselling one number — and the fastest way to win one is to raise the limitation before they do.

  • River level is modelled, never gauged. No public CWC feed exists.
  • The cloud read is not a rainfall forecast. It says the sky is organising and how fast. It is never blended with the rain forecast into one invented number.
  • Return periods are exceedance bands, not fitted curves. Five years of daily data holds five annual maxima — far too few for extreme-value fitting.
  • The disaster register and Census indicators are curated, not live. No free API carries Indian flood history, so every row shows its citation and source URL.
  • Satellite rasters are baked, not streamed. That is the deliberate trade that makes the app work with the network unplugged.
If a judge finds a limitation first

“Yes — and it's stated on that screen. Here.” Then show them. Never defend a number the app already qualifies.
