HAND is the height of a place above the nearest river. A zone 1.2 m above the water floods when the river rises 1.2 m — wherever it sits on the map. Compute it once from elevation, and you know the flooding order of an entire district before a single drop falls.

The problem

It is 2 a.m. in Darbhanga and the Bagmati is rising

The District Magistrate has to decide, right now, three things:

Which villages evacuate first. Where the twelve available boats go. Which roads still carry a truck.

Today that answer arrives by telephone. Someone rings the block office. The block office rings a village head. By the time a picture assembles, the water has moved.

Meanwhile satellites pass over that exact spot every few days, recording everything. That data is free, public, and almost entirely unused at the district level — because it arrives as a raster file for a remote-sensing analyst, not as an answer for the person holding the phone.

The physical insight

A flood happens under cloud. Cameras are blind exactly when you need them.

Optical satellites — the kind that take photographs — see white cloud and nothing else during a storm. That is why most flood imagery arrives days late, after the sky clears.

Radar does not care about cloud. Sentinel-1 fires C-band microwaves down and listens to the echo. Radio waves pass straight through weather, and work at night.

Water is smooth, so it reflects the pulse away from the satellite instead of back. The signature is unmistakable:

Water is black. Everything else is grey.

What we strip out

Raw radar over-calls water. Dry sand, smooth tarmac and radar shadow all read black. Three masks remove them: the permanent-water reference, a slope cut, and a HAND ceiling — ground too high above the river to be reachable by it.

Resolution and revisit

10 m pixels, 250 km swath — a whole district in one frame. Sentinel-1C restored the constellation in Dec 2024; most Indian districts now get a look every 2–6 days.

The gap

ISRO already maps floods. We are not competing with that.

Bhuvan and NDEM publish real flood layers, and they are good. They stop at a picture, delivered as a file, for a scientist. We consume their output and carry it the last five steps.

How it works

Ten stages, raw signal to a decision

Stages 1–2 run continuously over the whole country. Stages 3–10 run only on the districts that stage 2 flags.

01

Ingest

Cloud and rainfall every 30 min, from Open-Meteo with no API key. Radar granules every few days. River gauges are the one feed we cannot get: CWC publishes no public keyless API.gauges: no feed

02

Screen

Score all 735 districts every half hour: how far the cloud column is filled at low, mid and high level, whether high cloud runs ahead of low cloud into an anvil, convective energy, and rainfall against that district's own five-year record for the same week.built

03

Terrainbuilt

Elevation becomes HAND, wetness index and flow paths. Computed once per district, cached forever.

04

Detect

Radar before versus radar now. Grey that turned black is new water. Then the three masks strip the false positives.

05

Model

Between radar passes: rainfall to runoff to river stage to inundation, hourly. When a scene lands, the model snaps back to observed truth.

06

Depth

Water surface level minus ground height. 0.5 m means wade. 1.8 m means send a boat.

07

Expose

Overlay people, roads, hospitals, schools, cropland. Water in an empty field is a statistic; water in a village of four thousand is an emergency.

08

Rankthe answer

People × depth × how long it will stay × whether a truck can still reach. Sorted. The top of that list is where you go first.

09

Actthe differentiator

Boats, shelters with real capacity, alternate routes around cut roads, supply quantities from IRS norms, and a drafted SMS. Every rule traces to an NDMA SOP.

10

Deliver

Dashboard, CAP alert, or a printable situation report.

Does it scale

Watch the whole country. Model only what matters.

Nobody can run a 10-metre hydrological model across India continuously — the compute does not exist at any sane budget.

So we don't. A cheap screen runs over every district every half hour. The expensive model runs only where the screen fires.

That also gives the demo its opening shot: a map of India with districts lighting up, then a zoom into the one that matters today.

What we are honest about

The limits, stated before you find them

A pitch that claims no weaknesses is a pitch that has not been tested. These are ours, and each one has a stated mitigation rather than a shrug.

The most important: we are not primarily a warning system. The Assam floods of 2020 ran for six weeks. Nobody's problem was failing to know a flood was coming — it was not knowing which of 2,100 villages were under water that morning. That is a mapping and allocation problem, and a 2–6 day revisit solves it comfortably.

Revisit

10 m radar arrives every 2–6 days. Modelling fills the gap and is labelled as modelled. It does not eliminate it.

False +

Dry sand, smooth tarmac and radar shadow mimic water. Masked by permanent-water, slope and HAND filters.

Canopy

Flooding beneath dense forest and between tall buildings is genuinely hard for C-band. L-band NISAR helps.

Gauges

We have no live gauge feed at all. CWC runs roughly 200 stations but publishes no keyless API; three access routes were tried and documented. River stage is therefore routed from live rainfall through the DEM and labelled modelled on every response, never as a reading.

Flash

A four-hour Chennai flood will be missed by radar. That is what the 30-minute cloud screen exists for.

Census

Population is a 2011 census figure carried forward, not a live count. Labelled as an estimate everywhere.

Questions we expect

And the answers

"Bhuvan already does this."

Bhuvan gives an analyst a map. We give a District Magistrate a ranked task list. We consume Bhuvan's layers; we do not duplicate them.

"Six-day revisit means your data is stale."

For flash floods, correct — and we say so. For the riverine flooding that displaces millions in Assam and Bihar every year, events last weeks. The satellite comes back while the flood is still there.

"Is this real satellite data?"

Every algorithm is real and cited. The input runs live from Copernicus when keys are configured and simulated otherwise — and a permanent badge tells you which, on every screen. We would rather show you the badge than have a dead venue connection end the demo.

"Where is the AI?"

Deliberately not in the decision path. An evacuation order has to be defensible in an inquiry. Physics is auditable; a neural network's activation is not. ML refines thresholds and learns event patterns — it does not decide who evacuates.

"What does deployment actually look like?"

Python, FastAPI and NumPy on the backend; Leaflet on the front. No GPU, no GDAL, no build step. It runs on a district NIC server, or on a laptop in a field EOC with no internet.
